assignment-unit | Explain why the randomization unit determines which observations can be treated as independently assigned. | If a whole group receives one assignment, its members share that assignment; analysis must account for group dependence instead of counting each member as separately randomized. | Ten measurements from each assigned group create ten independent random assignments, even though every member received the same treatment together. | Connects assignment structure to dependence and the analysis unit. | Mistakes repeated observations within one assignment for independent assignments. | An experiment assigns treatment to a defined unit, which may be a person, task, machine or entire group. Multiple observations inside that unit do not create additional independent assignments. Preserve assignment identifiers in collected data and plan an analysis that respects their grouping. Reporting many measurements without their dependence structure can make uncertainty appear smaller than the design supports.
allocation-concealment | Explain why concealing upcoming assignments can protect an experiment before enrollment is complete. | If recruiters know the next assignment, they may influence who enters that arm; concealing it reduces selection based on anticipated treatment. | Announcing the entire upcoming assignment sequence improves randomization because recruiters can place participants into whichever arm seems most suitable. | Identifies selection before allocation as the relevant bias mechanism. | Endorses assignment-aware selection that defeats the randomized enrollment process. | Generating a random assignment sequence and concealing it are different design tasks. Knowledge of upcoming assignments can influence enrollment or task inclusion, even when the sequence itself was random. Keep future assignments unavailable to anyone who can select incoming units until eligibility is settled. Record exceptions and distinguish this protection from blinding outcomes after assignment.
attrition-comparison | Explain why removing participants who disappear after assignment can bias an arm comparison. | Loss after assignment may depend on the treatment or outcome, so comparing only remaining participants can compare selected groups rather than the original randomized groups. | Once randomization happens, any later removal is harmless because the original random process permanently guarantees comparable survivors. | Identifies outcome-related selection after assignment. | Assumes the initial assignment protects an arbitrarily selected later subset. | Follow-up loss can differ between experiment arms or depend on results that would have been observed. Dropping missing units without examining this mechanism can undermine the original comparison. Track every assigned unit, report losses and their reasons, and specify missing-data handling prospectively. Sensitivity analyses can show dependence on assumptions; they do not recover unobserved outcomes with certainty.
optional-stopping | Explain why repeatedly checking a conventional fixed-sample significance threshold and stopping at the first crossing changes its error behavior. | Each extra look creates another opportunity to cross by chance, so the planned fixed-sample error interpretation no longer applies unless the sequential procedure accounts for those looks. | A five-percent threshold has the same overall false-positive rate regardless of how often results are checked or when collection stops. | Explains repeated opportunities and the need for a sequentially valid procedure. | Treats a per-design threshold as invariant to an unplanned stopping rule. | A fixed-sample statistical procedure assumes its specified collection and analysis plan. Repeatedly looking and stopping on a favorable result changes that procedure. If sequential decisions are needed, choose an appropriate sequential design before collecting outcomes and report the looks that occurred. A predeclared stopping rule also makes time and cost decisions auditable instead of letting favorable fluctuations determine the sample.
outcome-selection | Explain why choosing the most favorable outcome after measuring many outcomes weakens an ordinary single-outcome inference. | Selection favors chance extremes among the measured outcomes, so the reported result needs the prespecified primary outcome or a method that accounts for the selection. | Measuring more outcomes can only add evidence; reporting whichever looks strongest retains the same single-outcome interpretation. | Identifies selective reporting as a change to the inference procedure. | Ignores that selecting an extreme result affects its chance behavior. | Before collection, distinguish primary outcomes from secondary or exploratory outcomes. Selecting the strongest-looking result from a large set is itself part of the analysis and changes the interpretation of an ordinary isolated threshold. Report what was measured and how selections were made. Exploratory findings can motivate a new experiment with a prospectively chosen outcome rather than being relabeled as the original test.
intention-to-treat | Explain what an intention-to-treat comparison preserves when some units do not follow their assigned intervention. | It compares groups according to assignment, preserving the randomized assignment contrast; it estimates the effect of offering that assignment rather than automatically the effect of perfect adherence. | Move every non-adherent unit to the arm it actually followed; that preserves randomization because the final behavior is what matters. | Defines the assignment-based comparison and limits its interpretation. | Reassigns units based on post-assignment behavior and calls that randomized. | Intention-to-treat analysis retains units in their assigned groups even when adherence differs. This preserves the assigned-policy comparison under the design's assumptions, but does not automatically estimate the effect of everyone following the intervention perfectly. Describe adherence separately and state the estimand. Analyses based on received treatment may answer other questions while requiring additional assumptions about selection into adherence.
baseline-balance | Explain why a chance baseline difference between randomized arms is possible without proving allocation manipulation. | Random assignment balances characteristics in expectation, not exactly in every finite sample; inspect the allocation process and use a prespecified adjustment rather than infer manipulation from imbalance alone. | Randomization guarantees identical baseline averages in every sample, so any observed difference proves the randomization failed. | Distinguishes expected balance from a deterministic finite-sample guarantee. | Treats a probabilistic design property as exact matching. | A finite randomized sample can show baseline differences by chance. Such differences may affect precision, but their existence alone does not establish a broken assignment procedure. Audit the actual allocation mechanism and report important baseline characteristics. Plan justified adjustment or stratification prospectively where possible. Do not repeatedly rerandomize until an attractive result appears without defining and accounting for that design.
interference | Explain why one unit's intervention affecting another unit can invalidate a simple no-interference treatment contrast. | Outcomes may depend on other units' assignments as well as their own, so the design must account for spillovers or define a policy effect that includes them. | Random labels make spillovers impossible; a treated unit changing a control unit's environment cannot affect the comparison. | Identifies the violated dependence assumption and a design-level response. | Claims assignment randomization prevents physical or behavioral spillovers. | Treatment can change shared environments, communication or resources, causing effects across assigned units. A simple comparison that assumes each outcome depends only on its own assignment may then answer a different question from the intended isolated effect. Anticipate plausible spillovers and choose grouping or exposure definitions accordingly. Keep the effect of the assignment policy distinct from an assumed effect on an isolated unit.
crossover-carryover | Explain why a crossover experiment can need a washout or a model of carryover between conditions. | Exposure in the first period may affect outcomes in the second, so changing the label alone does not reset the unit; allow justified washout or account for persistent effects. | Giving each unit both conditions automatically cancels every lasting effect, regardless of order or time between periods. | Describes persistent exposure and its consequence for the later comparison. | Assumes within-unit comparison erases temporal dependence. | Crossover designs compare conditions within the same unit, but earlier exposure may influence later outcomes. Counterbalancing order helps with some order effects and does not by itself remove every carryover mechanism. Decide whether a justified washout is possible and whether the intended estimand remains meaningful. Record period and sequence, and test the design on tasks where the unit can genuinely return to the relevant starting condition.
measurement-blinding | Explain how knowing the assigned arm can influence subjective outcome assessment even with valid randomization. | Assessors may interpret ambiguous outcomes differently by arm, so blind assessment or a sufficiently objective prospective rubric reduces that measurement pathway. | Randomization prevents assessors from favoring an arm, so revealing treatment labels cannot affect subjective scoring. | Separates allocation validity from bias in measuring the outcome. | Assumes random allocation controls the assessor's later judgment. | Randomization controls assignment, not every later measurement decision. When an assessor knows the arm, borderline outcomes can be interpreted differently. Blind assessors where feasible, provide prospective acceptance criteria and record adjudication. When blinding is impossible, disclose that limitation and use objective measurements where they answer the question. Agreement with an expected result is not evidence that an unblinded judgment was unbiased.
